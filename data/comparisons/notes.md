# Synthesis notes: what drove planetary-defense funding and attention

These are working notes, not a peer-reviewed history. Claims are sourced where I found direct documentation (mostly NASA/Goddard and NASA Science pages, SpaceNews, and the IAWN/SMPAG institutional record); anywhere I could not pin down a primary source, I have flagged it explicitly rather than asserting it.

## (a) Shoemaker-Levy 9 and the birth of "planetary defense" as a funded program

Before July 1994, "planetary defense" was not an institutional category. NEO search work existed (Eugene Shoemaker and colleagues had been advocating for a systematic survey since the 1980s, and the 1992 NASA-commissioned "Spaceguard Survey" report by the Morrison working group had already proposed a ten-year, ground-based telescope network to find 90% of NEOs larger than 1 km), but it had no dedicated federal funding line and little public visibility. Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 — discovered in March 1993 by Carolyn and Eugene Shoemaker and David Levy, already broken by Jupiter's tides into a chain of more than 20 fragments — struck Jupiter over six days in July 1994. The impacts were not merely detected; they were watched in near-real time by Hubble and ground observatories worldwide, producing plumes thousands of kilometers high and atmospheric scars larger than Earth that lasted for months. That visual, visceral evidence — a "practice run" showing a giant planet being battered by a body that would have been civilization-ending at Earth — is what changed the politics. By 1998, Congress had directed NASA to find 90% of near-Earth asteroids ≥1 km in diameter within ten years (the "Spaceguard goal"), essentially adopting the 1992 Spaceguard Survey recommendation but now with real funding and a deadline. NASA met that goal by the end of 2010. NASA's own retrospective explicitly credits SL9 as the catalyst: Heidi Hammel (who led the visible-light Hubble observations) is quoted saying the event "invigorated our understanding of how important it is to monitor our local neighborhood," and Lindley Johnson — who was advocating for NEO tracking within the U.S. Air Force in the mid-1990s and later became NASA's first Planetary Defense Officer — says SL9 "showed us that we are vulnerable to impacts in the present day, not just in the distant past." I could not find a single authoritative document laying out the exact congressional appropriations mechanism (committee, bill number, hearing dates) that produced the 1998 mandate; NASA and SpaceNews retrospectives describe it as congressional direction "influenced by" Shoemaker's advocacy and the Hubble imagery, but I did not verify the specific legislative vehicle. Flagged as unverified: the precise budget dollar figures attached to the 1998-2010 Spaceguard survey effort.

## (b) Chelyabinsk and the second wave: international coordination despite an existing survey program

By February 2013, the original 1998 Spaceguard goal (1 km and larger objects) had already been essentially met — which is precisely what made Chelyabinsk notable: the survey system built in response to SL9 was not designed to catch objects in the ~20 m class, and indeed it did not. Chelyabinsk's real influence was institutional and international rather than "starting the program" — it accelerated and internationalized what already existed, and shifted the target size downward. The remarkable coincidence, confirmed by NASA's own account, is that the UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) Working Group on Near-Earth Objects was meeting in Vienna on the very day of the Chelyabinsk airburst to finalize recommendations for international asteroid-defense coordination. That timing gave the recommendations unusual force: COPUOS endorsed them in June 2013, and the UN General Assembly formally endorsed the creation of two bodies in December 2013 — the International Asteroid Warning Network (IAWN), for worldwide detection/tracking collaboration among observatories and surveys, and the Space Mission Planning Advisory Group (SMPAG), a forum for space agencies to coordinate deflection-mission planning. IAWN's steering committee held its first meeting in January 2014; SMPAG met for the first time later that year. Within NASA specifically, Chelyabinsk is credited (NASA's own five-years-later retrospective, and statements from Lindley Johnson) with catalyzing the January 2016 establishment of the Planetary Defense Coordination Office (PDCO) as a formal program office, continued growth in NEO discovery rates (NASA reports a 84% increase in known NEOs between January 2013 and January 2018), and concrete operational milestones like the October 2017 global observing exercise on the close approach of asteroid 2012 TC4 — the first real-world test of the international detect/track/communicate pipeline built after 2013. It also lent political support to what became the DART kinetic-impactor demonstration mission. Flagged as not independently verified in my research pass: specific dollar-for-dollar NASA planetary-defense budget figures before vs. after 2013 (NASA materials describe program growth in qualitative and discovery-count terms, not a clean budget time series I could confirm from a primary budget document).

## (c) The general pattern: dramatic events as punctuation, not the whole story

Both cases fit a consistent pattern of "punctuated" policy attention layered on top of continuous, slower scientific and technical progress. The underlying capability — larger telescopes, better CCDs/detectors, automated moving-object pipelines, orbit-determination software — advanced steadily across decades regardless of any single event; Spacewatch, LINEAR, NEAT, Catalina Sky Survey, Pan-STARRS, and ATLAS were each funded and built through ordinary program cycles, not solely as crisis responses. But the *scale, deadlines, and international scope* of what got funded and coordinated tracks closely with dramatic, visually or physically undeniable events: SL9 gave policymakers an image of consequence at planetary scale that turned an existing scientific recommendation (the 1992 Spaceguard report) into a funded mandate; Chelyabinsk gave a real, populated-Earth casualty event that turned an already-successful large-object survey into a smaller-object, internationally coordinated one. In both cases the "surprise" was not that impacts happen — scientists already knew and had already proposed responses — but that decision-makers and the public had not internalized the risk until they had a vivid, immediate example. This is consistent with a broader pattern documented elsewhere in impact-hazard literature and policy history: risk perception and resource allocation for low-probability/high-consequence hazards tend to move in response to salient events rather than in proportion to expert risk estimates, and each "waking" event tends to close a specific, previously-underweighted gap (kilometer-scale objects after SL9; decameter-scale, populated-area risk and international coordination after Chelyabinsk) rather than uniformly raising resources at all size scales at once. I have not attempted to verify this last generalization against a dedicated risk-perception or science-policy study; it is offered here as a pattern observed across the two cases documented above, not as an independently sourced claim.
